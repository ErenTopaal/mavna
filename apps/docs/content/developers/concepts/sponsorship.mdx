# Sponsorship

A Stellar account cannot hold USDC without a trustline, a trustline costs a
reserve in XLM, and a user arriving from another chain has no XLM. Sponsorship is
how Mavna completes that transfer anyway: a funded sponsor fronts the deposit, and
the deposit repays itself out of the arriving money.

One idea governs the mechanism: **the deposit is a loan, and the repayment is
signed before the loan is made.** Everything below is that idea in order.

## What it costs

Stellar locks a deposit per unit of ledger space, read from the ledger rather than
hardcoded: 0.5 XLM per unit at the current base reserve. An account occupies two
units and each trustline one, so a new account able to hold one asset locks 1.5
XLM. The quote carries this as an `account-reserve` fee line, a kind of its own,
because it is not a fee anyone takes: the XLM ends up locked in the user's own
account and returns to them if they close it.

`inspectDestination` in `@mavna/stellar` classifies what a destination needs in
one read: nothing (an account that can take the asset now, any account for XLM,
or a contract deployed on this network), a trustline only, or a whole account
plus a trustline (only the account, for XLM). It reads as unusable, with the
reason, when no contract exists at a contract address, or when a trustline exists
but the issuer has not authorised it or its limit has no room left (or no room
for the amount, when one is given). Funds sent to a contract address with nothing
deployed behind it can never be moved, so that case is refused rather than
treated as ready. Only the trustline and account cases cost the sponsor anything,
and the routing layer knows which before it quotes.

Transfers that need sponsoring have a floor, checked at quote time: four times the
deposit's value, converted at the live XLM price from Stellar's own orderbook,
averaged over the first 1,000 XLM of each side so a stray small order inside the
spread cannot move it.
Below the bare deposit, recovery is impossible, because there is not enough
arriving to buy the deposit back; the multiple gives headroom above that line. A
transfer under the floor is refused with a reason that names the minimum and
offers the alternative of sending to an address that already exists.

## The lifecycle, in order

The ordering is the design. Each step exists because of a specific failure the
order prevents.

**1. Settlement first, before anything moves.** While the user is present, the
receiving wallet connects, proving it owns the destination, and signs one
transaction that repays the deposit out of the arriving funds. It is a path
payment that buys back exactly the XLM the reserve needs, spending at most twice
its value at the live rate, so the user's exposure is a number they read before
signing. It is a strict-receive payment, so a moving market is absorbed by the
maximum spend rather than invalidating a signature already collected. The signed
settlement is held, unsubmitted; the funds it spends do not exist yet.

Collecting this first closes the case where someone receives and walks away. The
[sponsor service](/developers/sponsor) enforces it: it refuses to submit any
preparation for an account it holds no settlement for.

**2. The burn.** The user signs the source-chain transactions and the transfer
enters the rail. No sponsor capital is committed yet.

**3. Preparation, as late as possible.** Only after the rail's attestation exists,
meaning the transfer is certain to be deliverable, does the sponsor commit
capital: one transaction, sourced and paid by the sponsor, that creates the
account with a zero starting balance and opens the trustline, with the sponsorship
operations wrapped around it. The user co-signs the operations that create
obligations on their own account, at no cost to them.

Lateness is load-bearing. Every second between committing the deposit and the
money arriving is a window in which a failed bridge turns the deposit into a loss.
Preparing after attestation shrinks that window from the bridge's minutes to
seconds. The builder opens at most two trustlines per account, which is what stops
an attacker inducing an unbounded number of sponsored trustlines.

**4. Delivery, then settlement submits itself.** The mint lands in the prepared
account, the held settlement is countersigned and submitted in the same breath,
the user's arrival buys the deposit back, and the sponsor's capital is released
for the next person. The user owns the account outright and nobody is owed
anything.

## What failure costs, and who pays

Every failure mode lands on the sponsor, never on the user. A settlement signed
but never sent commits nothing. A delivery that lands while its settlement cannot
leaves the user with everything that arrived and the sponsor still sponsoring,
retrying the held settlement until its time bound. The one loss the design carries
is a bridge that fails after the sponsor has prepared, bounded by the small window
above.

Two daily caps bound the sponsor's exposure, checked before anyone is asked for a
signature. [The sponsor service](/developers/sponsor) covers them.

## Where this runs

`buildPreparation` and `buildSettlement` live in `@mavna/stellar` and return
unsigned transactions; they never sign. The funded key, the held settlements, the
caps, and the delivery trigger live in the [sponsor service](/developers/sponsor),
a small self-hostable process whose entire authority is listed per endpoint. Run
your own and none of Mavna's limits are yours.
